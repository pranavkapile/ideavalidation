Step 01 / 12 · Idea Capture & Problem Identification

Capture the raw idea and trace it back to a real problem

Turn a vague concept into a falsifiable problem statement. The goal is not to validate your idea — it's to identify exactly what pain or inefficiency you believe exists, and why you believe it.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What is the exact problem you are solving? Write it in one sentence without mentioning your solution.
  • Q→Who experiences this problem and how often? Daily friction vs. rare edge case?
  • Q→How do people currently cope with this problem? What workarounds exist?
  • Q→Why does this problem exist? Is it systemic, behavioural, technological, or regulatory?
  • Q→What is the cost of the problem not being solved — time, money, stress, missed opportunity?
  • Q→Why are you specifically interested in this problem? Do you have an unfair advantage here?
  • Q→Is this problem getting worse over time, staying the same, or fading away?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Write a single problem statement: "I notice that [WHO] struggle to [DO WHAT] because [ROOT CAUSE], which results in [CONSEQUENCE]."
  • ✓
    List all assumptions baked into that statement — explicitly name them.
  • ✓
    Identify 3–5 real people who might have this problem. Do not use friends unless they are genuine target users.
  • ✓
    Conduct 3 informal "problem conversations" — ask about the pain, not your solution. Record verbatim responses.
  • ✓
    Search forums (Reddit, Quora, LinkedIn, industry forums) for complaints or workarounds related to this problem.
  • ✓
    Rate the problem on 3 axes: Frequency (how often), Severity (how bad), Urgency (how badly people want it solved now).
Validation Criteria
Problem Statement
You can write one clean sentence describing the problem without mentioning your solution.
Problem Confirmed
At least 2 of 3 real people confirmed the problem exists in their own words.
Real Cost Exists
The problem has a quantifiable cost: time, money, missed outcome, or emotional burden.
Evidence Online
Forum threads or reviews show strangers complaining about this without being prompted.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedProblem is real, confirmed by multiple independent sources, and has measurable cost.
↻ RefineProblem exists but framing is too vague or broad — tighten the scope.
⇄ PivotPeople describe a different, adjacent problem. Follow their words, not your assumptions.
✕ KillNobody confirmed the problem. Your evidence is 100% self-generated. Start over.
Step 02 / 12 · Problem Clarity

Sharpen the problem until it can't be misread

Most founders are solving a symptom, not a root cause. This step forces you to dig deeper with structured analysis until you understand exactly what you're dealing with — and what you're not.

Critical Trap Founders almost always overestimate how well they understand the problem. Keep questioning until the answers stop surprising you — and they will surprise you.
Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What is the root cause? Apply the "5 Whys" — ask why 5 times in a row and document each layer.
  • Q→What is the exact context in which this problem occurs — time, place, trigger event?
  • Q→What emotion does the person feel when experiencing this problem? Frustration? Helplessness? Embarrassment?
  • Q→Is this a universal problem or does it only apply in specific conditions (geography, company size, industry)?
  • Q→Who benefits from this problem existing? (Incumbents, middlemen, regulators — they'll resist your solution.)
  • Q→Has anyone tried to solve this before and failed? What did they get wrong?
  • Q→What would the perfect world look like for the person experiencing this problem?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Apply the "5 Whys" to your problem statement. Document each layer. The fifth answer is your real problem.
  • ✓
    Draw the problem journey: what happens before, during, and after the pain point? Where exactly does it break?
  • ✓
    Conduct 5 more structured customer discovery interviews (20–30 min). Focus on the past: "Tell me about the last time this happened."
  • ✓
    Document the most surprising thing you heard. If nothing surprised you, your questions weren't deep enough.
  • ✓
    Identify whether this is a "hair-on-fire" problem (urgent, desperate) or a nice-to-have. Only the former justifies a startup.
  • ✓
    Write a revised problem statement incorporating what you've learned. If it hasn't changed, you haven't learned anything.
Validation Criteria
Root Cause Identified
You've traced the problem past surface symptoms to a structural cause.
Hair on Fire
At least 3 interviewees described this as urgent enough that they actively seek a better solution.
Consistent Pattern
Multiple unconnected people describe the same problem in similar language without being led.
Stakes Are Real
The cost is significant enough to justify paying for or switching behaviour.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedProblem is deep, urgent, and consistently described across interviews. Root cause is clear.
↻ RefineProblem is real but scope is unclear — can't tell if it's a feature or a company. Narrow further.
⇄ PivotDiscovery revealed a related but more painful problem. Reframe around that instead.
✕ KillThe problem is trivial, infrequent, or accepted as normal with no desire to change.
Step 03 / 12 · Customer Identification

Define exactly who you're building for — and who you're not

Vague customer definitions kill startups. "SMBs" is not a customer. "Marketing managers at SaaS companies with 50–500 employees managing paid acquisition budgets over €50k/month" is a customer. This step forces precision.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→Who experiences this problem most acutely? Not broadly — specifically.
  • Q→Who has both the problem AND the ability to pay for a solution?
  • Q→Who is your "early adopter" — the person most desperate for a solution right now, even if imperfect?
  • Q→Who makes the purchase decision vs. who uses the product? Are they the same person?
  • Q→What does a day in their life look like? Where do they spend time, who do they talk to, what tools do they use?
  • Q→How large is this customer segment? Is it large enough to build a business on?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Write a one-paragraph Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) with demographic and psychographic specifics. No weasel words like "anyone who…"
  • ✓
    List 3 distinct customer segments. Rank them by: pain severity, willingness to pay, ease to reach, and market size.
  • ✓
    Pick ONE primary segment to focus on. Justify with evidence, not preference.
  • ✓
    Identify 10 specific real people or companies matching your ICP. Name them. If you can't name 10, the segment isn't defined enough.
  • ✓
    Map the decision-making process: who discovers the problem, evaluates solutions, approves the purchase, and uses it daily.
  • ✓
    Estimate TAM/SAM/SOM using bottoms-up math, not top-down percentages.
Validation Criteria
Specificity
ICP is specific enough that you could find 100 matching people on LinkedIn in under an hour.
Pain + Budget
Target customer both feels the pain acutely AND has a budget or willingness to pay.
Reachable
You can identify a channel to reach this customer without a massive marketing budget.
Market Size
SOM in Year 1 is a real revenue number. SAM is large enough to support scale.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedNamed, specific, reachable segment with confirmed pain and ability to pay.
↻ RefineSegment too broad. Narrow until you can name specific companies or people.
⇄ PivotA different segment has more pain or more budget. Shift primary focus.
✕ KillNo specific segment has both the pain and the purchasing power.

Step 04 / 12 · Market Need Validation

Prove the market wants this — not just that people say they do

People lie in surveys. What matters is what they do with their time, money, and attention. This step forces you to find behavioural evidence of demand, not just stated preference.

The "Mom Test" Trap If you're asking "Would you use this?" you're doing it wrong. Ask about existing behaviour: What do they currently do? What have they already paid for? What did they try that failed? Actions reveal truth; words reveal politeness.
Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→Are people already spending money on inferior solutions? What are they spending?
  • Q→Are there active communities (subreddits, Slack groups, forums) where people discuss this problem and seek advice?
  • Q→What is the search volume for this problem? Are people actively looking for solutions online?
  • Q→Have any companies tried and failed in this space? What was their failure mode?
  • Q→Is this a growing problem (tailwinds exist) or a shrinking one? What macro trend drives it?
  • Q→Can you get someone to pay you — even a small amount — before building anything? Document all attempts.
Action Items
  • ✓
    Run Google Keyword Planner or Ahrefs on 10 search queries related to your problem. Document monthly volumes and trends.
  • ✓
    Spend 2 hours on Reddit, Quora, LinkedIn, G2, Trustpilot. Find 20 real complaints about this problem. Screenshot them.
  • ✓
    Create a "fake door" landing page and measure sign-up conversion rate. Minimum threshold: 5% of visitors convert.
  • ✓
    Attempt to pre-sell: message 20 target customers with a 1-paragraph pitch. Count genuine "when can I get this?" responses.
  • ✓
    Research market size using industry reports or comparable company revenues. Cross-check bottoms-up vs. top-down.
  • ✓
    Interview 3 people who tried existing solutions and abandoned them. What was the final straw? What would make them switch?
Validation Criteria
Behavioural Demand
People already spend money or time on inferior solutions — not just say they'd consider yours.
Organic Evidence
Strangers publicly complain about this problem and seek solutions without knowing you exist.
Pre-Sell Signal
At least 2–3 people responded positively to a cold pre-sell pitch or joined a waitlist.
Trend Direction
The problem is growing, not shrinking. A macro tailwind drives demand.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedBehavioural evidence, organic demand signals, and at least 1 pre-sell or conversion exists.
↻ RefineSignals are weak. Run more pre-sell tests or narrow the segment to find a hotter pocket of demand.
⇄ PivotDemand exists for a slightly different solution. Adjust the solution direction.
✕ KillNo behavioural evidence of demand. People only give verbal interest. Nobody acts.
Step 05 / 12 · Competitor Analysis

Understand what you're walking into — and who will fight back

If you think you have no competitors, you don't understand your market. Competition validates demand. Lack of competition may signal a dead end.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→Who else is solving this problem — directly, indirectly, or partially?
  • Q→What are the top 3 existing solutions? What do customers like and hate about them?
  • Q→What is the incumbents' business model, pricing, and go-to-market strategy?
  • Q→Where do competitors fall short — by design, by choice, or by constraint?
  • Q→If a large player wanted to copy your idea tomorrow, how quickly could they? What would stop them?
  • Q→What do customers say in 1-star reviews of competitor products? (This is your feature roadmap.)
Action Items
  • ✓
    List every direct and indirect competitor. Minimum 5 total.
  • ✓
    For the top 3: document pricing, key features, customer reviews, funding stage, and team size.
  • ✓
    Read 1-star and 2-star reviews of competitor products. This is your feature roadmap and positioning brief in one document.
  • ✓
    Build a 2x2 positioning matrix. Place each competitor on it. Identify a white space where you can own a position.
  • ✓
    Sign up for and trial the top 2 competitor products for one week. Document exactly where they fail your ICP.
  • ✓
    Identify what moats competitors have built (network effects, switching costs, IP, data, brand). Assess your ability to overcome each.
Validation Criteria
Gap Identified
A specific, documented weakness in existing solutions that your ICP consistently complains about.
Moat Awareness
You understand competitor moats and can articulate how you'd survive despite them.
Market Proof
Competitors with funding confirm the market is real. You're not first — you're better.
Positioning Space
A defensible, uncrowded position exists on your 2x2 matrix.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedCompetition validates demand and you've identified a clear gap you can credibly own.
↻ RefineGap identified but can't articulate why you'd win. Sharpen differentiation before continuing.
⇄ PivotGap exists in a different segment. Adjust your ICP accordingly.
✕ KillCompetitors too entrenched, or no competition because there's no market. Either way, stop.
Step 06 / 12 · Unique Value Proposition

Define exactly why someone would choose you over every alternative

Your UVP is not a tagline. It's the single most important strategic decision you'll make. It must be specific, defensible, and immediately understood by your target customer.

Common UVP Failures "Faster, easier, cheaper" is not a UVP. "AI-powered" is not a UVP. "All-in-one platform" is not a UVP. A real UVP answers: "Why would your exact ICP choose you over doing nothing or using the incumbent — in one sentence a 10-year-old could understand?"
Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What specific outcome does your ICP get that they can't get anywhere else?
  • Q→If you removed every feature except one, what is the single thing that makes this indispensable?
  • Q→Can you quantify the value? How much time, money, or effort does your customer save or gain?
  • Q→What is your unfair advantage — something competitors can't easily replicate?
  • Q→Write your UVP using this format: "For [WHO], [PRODUCT] is the only [CATEGORY] that [SPECIFIC OUTCOME] because [PROOF/MECHANISM]."
  • Q→List 3 ways a competitor could replicate your UVP within 12 months. How do you defend against each?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Test your UVP with 5 target customers: read it to them, then ask "What does that mean to you?" If they can't paraphrase it, rewrite it.
  • ✓
    Challenge your own UVP: list 3 ways a competitor could replicate it within 12 months. If all seem plausible, you need a deeper moat.
  • ✓
    Identify your unfair advantage (founder insight, proprietary data, network, tech, distribution).
  • ✓
    Create a comparison table: you vs. top 3 alternatives across 5–7 dimensions that matter to your ICP. Your UVP should be visually obvious.
  • ✓
    Quantify the value wherever possible. "Saves 4 hours per week" beats "saves time."
Validation Criteria
Clarity Test
A stranger from your ICP can accurately describe what you do after one reading.
Differentiation
You can name exactly one thing you do that no direct competitor does for the same ICP.
Defensibility
At least one structural reason competitors can't immediately copy your advantage.
Customer Language
Your UVP uses words your ICP actually uses — not yours.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedUVP is specific, quantified, defensible, and confirmed by ICP in their own words.
↻ RefineUVP is weak or generic. Return to customer interviews to find the resonant language.
⇄ PivotStrongest differentiation is in a different area. Rebuild UVP around your actual advantage.
✕ KillYou cannot articulate a single compelling reason to choose you over alternatives. This is fatal.

Step 07 / 12 · Technical & Operational Feasibility

Assess whether you have — or can get — what it takes to build this

Feasibility isn't about whether the product is theoretically buildable. It's about whether your team can build it within the time and capital you have, at a quality level that satisfies your ICP.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What does the minimum viable version require technically? What skills, infrastructure, and tools?
  • Q→Does your team have these skills in-house? If not, what is the realistic cost and timeline to acquire them?
  • Q→Are there regulatory, legal, or compliance barriers? (Healthcare, finance, data privacy, GDPR, industry-specific.)
  • Q→What key dependencies exist — APIs, data providers, partnerships — and what happens if they change terms?
  • Q→How long will it realistically take to ship an MVP a real customer would pay for?
  • Q→What is the single biggest technical or operational risk that could kill this project?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Write a plain-English technical spec of the MVP. Break it into components. Estimate time and skills needed for each.
  • ✓
    Identify every external dependency. For each: what's your fallback if it breaks, gets expensive, or disappears?
  • ✓
    Research all relevant regulations. If applicable, consult a lawyer for 1 hour — cost is minimal vs. the risk of building on shaky legal ground.
  • ✓
    Honestly audit your team's capability gaps. Provide a concrete plan (hire, outsource, learn) for each gap.
  • ✓
    Create a realistic build timeline with a 50% buffer for unknowns. If that takes you to cash-zero, reduce scope or raise capital.
  • ✓
    Identify the single biggest technical assumption. Design the cheapest possible test to validate it before committing to a full build.
Validation Criteria
Buildable
MVP can be built with available skills or affordable hires within 3–6 months.
Legal Clarity
No unresolved legal or compliance blocker that could prevent launch or require significant rework.
Dependency Risk
No single external dependency can kill the product without a viable alternative.
Timeline Reality
MVP ships before runway ends, at a quality level customers would actually pay for.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedMVP is buildable within realistic constraints. No fatal blockers exist.
↻ RefineGaps exist but are fillable. Hire, reduce MVP scope, or partner before proceeding.
⇄ PivotFull product isn't feasible but a simpler version of the core value is. Build that instead.
✕ KillCritical capability gap, regulatory blocker, or impossible timeline makes execution infeasible.
Step 08 / 12 · Business Model Validation

Prove the unit economics work before you build anything

A good product with a broken business model is a charity. This step forces you to stress-test how money flows in and out of the business at unit level — before you spend a cent on development.

The CAC Delusion Founders consistently underestimate CAC by 3–5x and overestimate LTV by the same. Build your model using pessimistic CAC and conservative churn assumptions, then ask if the business still works. If it only works when everything goes right, it won't work.
Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What is your revenue model — subscription, transaction fee, usage-based, marketplace, licensing?
  • Q→What price will customers actually pay? How did you test this — not just ask?
  • Q→What is your realistic Cost to Acquire a Customer (CAC) via your primary channel?
  • Q→What is the average customer lifetime? What drives churn and how do you contain it?
  • Q→What is your LTV:CAC ratio? (Must exceed 3:1 to be investable.) What is your gross margin?
  • Q→What is the single assumption in your business model that, if wrong, kills the business?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Build a unit economics model in a spreadsheet: Price → COGS → Gross Profit → CAC → LTV → Payback Period. No verbal estimates.
  • ✓
    Run 3 pricing sensitivity tests at 3 different price points with 10 target customers each. Use Willingness-to-Pay framework.
  • ✓
    Estimate CAC using industry benchmarks for your primary channel. Add 100% buffer to be conservative.
  • ✓
    Stress-test: what happens if CAC doubles? If monthly churn is 10%? If price must drop 30%? Does the model survive?
  • ✓
    Complete the full Lean Canvas — all 9 blocks. Every empty block is a gap in your thinking.
  • ✓
    Design the fastest, cheapest test to validate your most dangerous business model assumption.
Validation Criteria
LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1
Lifetime value exceeds acquisition cost by at least 3x. Calculated with real data, not guesses.
Payback <18 Months
CAC is recovered within 18 months at realistic pricing and retention.
Gross Margin ≥ 50%
Sufficient margin to eventually fund growth without perpetual external capital.
Stress-Test Survival
Business model remains viable under 2 simultaneous pessimistic assumptions.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedUnit economics work under conservative assumptions. Clear path to profitability exists.
↻ RefineEconomics are marginal. Test higher pricing, lower-CAC channels, or reduce COGS before committing.
⇄ PivotRevenue model is wrong. Customers won't pay in this structure. Change the model, not the product.
✕ KillUnit economics don't work even under optimistic assumptions. No realistic path to profitability.
Step 09 / 12 · Risk Analysis

Name every risk before it names you

Risk analysis is operational intelligence. The founders who survive anticipated threats and built responses before being forced to improvise under pressure.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What are the top 5 reasons this fails? Be brutally specific — not "competition" but "Salesforce adds this as a native feature in 6 months."
  • Q→What external forces could make your solution irrelevant — regulation change, technology shift, market collapse?
  • Q→What is your biggest team risk — single point of failure, skills gap, co-founder conflict?
  • Q→What happens if your primary customer acquisition channel disappears overnight?
  • Q→What is your runway, and what happens if revenue is 6 months behind plan?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Run a Pre-Mortem: "It's 18 months from now and this failed completely. Write the story of why." Turn each cause into a mitigation.
  • ✓
    Create a Risk Register: list all risks with Probability (H/M/L), Impact (Fatal/Serious/Minor), and a specific mitigation for each.
  • ✓
    Identify your 3 critical dependencies. For each: what's the backup plan if it fails or gets 3x more expensive?
  • ✓
    Map financial risk: months of runway, break-even revenue, and your plan if you're 50% off targets at Month 6.
  • ✓
    Identify 1–2 black-swan risks. Decide explicitly whether to accept, mitigate, or transfer (insurance/legal) each one.
  • ✓
    Review your risk register with a skeptic. Their objections are gifts.
Validation Criteria
Pre-Mortem Done
Failure story written and each cause converted into a concrete mitigation or early warning signal.
Risk Register Complete
Every high-impact risk has a specific, actionable response — not "we'll deal with it later."
Runway Clarity
You know exactly how many months of runway you have and what triggers a fundraise or pivot decision.
No Hidden Killers
No risk is listed as "low priority" simply because it's uncomfortable to confront.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedAll high-impact risks have mitigations. No single risk can end the business without a recovery path.
↻ RefineRisk register incomplete or mitigations are theoretical. Build real responses before moving forward.
⇄ PivotRisks concentrated in one area — adjust strategy to reduce exposure before committing.
✕ KillMultiple fatal risks with no viable mitigation exist simultaneously. Probability of survival too low.

Step 10 / 12 · MVP Definition

Define the smallest thing you can build that tests your core thesis

Your MVP is not a beta product. It's a learning instrument. It exists to test the riskiest assumption in your model as quickly and cheaply as possible. Every feature that doesn't test that assumption is waste.

The MVP Myth Most "MVPs" are mini-products — polished, full-featured, and built over 6 months. A real MVP can be a spreadsheet, a Notion doc, a concierge service, or a manual process wrapped in a UI. If you can test your hypothesis without code, do that first.
Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→What is the single most critical assumption your business is built on? This is what your MVP must test.
  • Q→What is the minimum set of features that proves or disproves that assumption?
  • Q→Can you test the assumption without writing code? If yes — describe how.
  • Q→Write your MVP success statement: "Our MVP will prove/disprove that [ASSUMPTION] by [METHOD] with [METRIC] within [TIMEFRAME]."
  • Q→Who are your first 10 MVP users? List them by name or role. If you can't name 10, your go-to-market is already broken.
  • Q→What is your hard deadline and budget cap for the MVP phase?
Action Items
  • ✓
    List all planned features. For each: "Does this test our core assumption?" If not, cut it from V1.
  • ✓
    Choose MVP type: Concierge (manual delivery), Wizard of Oz (fake automation), Landing Page, Prototype, or No-Code. Pick cheapest option that generates real signal.
  • ✓
    Reach out to your first 10 MVP users individually. If you can't recruit 10, your GTM is already broken.
  • ✓
    Define success threshold in advance: "We proceed if X of Y users do Z within W days." Do not change this after seeing results.
  • ✓
    Set a hard deadline and budget cap. Build and test within those constraints, or not at all.
Validation Criteria
Core Test Defined
MVP tests exactly one critical assumption. Success/fail criteria written before building starts.
Scope Minimal
Every feature directly linked to testing the core assumption. Nothing included "just in case."
Users Identified
10 specific users committed to using and providing feedback on the MVP.
Timeline Constrained
Hard deadline set. MVP ships within 4–8 weeks maximum.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedMVP is scoped, users are lined up, success criteria are written, timeline is set. Build it.
↻ RefineMVP still too large. Cut until one assumption remains.
⇄ PivotCan't recruit MVP users — ICP doesn't care enough. Reconsider the problem or the customer.
✕ KillCheapest possible MVP still requires too much time or capital before runway ends.
Step 11 / 12 · Testing & Feedback Loops

Run structured tests. Separate signal from noise. Iterate with discipline.

Feedback is only valuable if collected and interpreted with rigour. Unsolicited praise is noise. Unprompted return behaviour is signal.

Key Questions & Answers
  • Q→Are users returning without being prompted to? What is your Week 1 and Week 2 retention rate?
  • Q→What is the exact moment users get value from the product — the "aha moment"? How long does it take to reach it?
  • Q→Where do users drop off or get confused? What does your funnel look like?
  • Q→What are users doing that you didn't expect — both positively and negatively?
  • Q→What do your churned users have in common? What do your best-retained users have in common?
  • Q→What is your current NPS? What do the open-ended comments say?
Action Items
  • ✓
    Watch 5 users use your MVP without helping or explaining. Document every moment of hesitation or abandonment. Do not interrupt.
  • ✓
    Track one north star metric that measures whether users are getting core value. Review weekly.
  • ✓
    Run weekly 20-min interviews with active users: "What did you use it for this week? Most frustrating moment? What would you miss if it disappeared?"
  • ✓
    Survey churned users within 48 hours: "What made you stop?" This is more valuable than any other data source.
  • ✓
    Run at least 2 full build-measure-learn cycles. Each must change something and measure the result.
  • ✓
    Document every learning regardless of whether it supports or contradicts your hypothesis. Honest logs beat selective memory.
Validation Criteria
Retention Signal
Users return on their own, unprompted, at least once within the first week.
Paying Customers
At least 3 users have paid real money, or committed to pay at launch.
Aha Moment ID'd
You know which action correlates with a user becoming retained. This drives your onboarding.
Learning Velocity
Each cycle teaches something new. If you're not being surprised, you're not testing deeply enough.
Decision Gate
✓ ProceedRetention exists, people are paying, core value is proven, and you know exactly why people come back.
↻ IterateEngagement exists but retention doesn't. Keep iterating on onboarding and the time-to-value path.
⇄ PivotA different use case is getting traction than planned. Follow the retention data, not your roadmap.
✕ KillNo user has returned without prompting. No user has paid. Fundamental demand is not there.
Step 12 / 12 · Final Validation & Go / No-Go Decision

Make a clear, evidence-backed decision. No hedging.

This is a court verdict, not a committee discussion. You've collected evidence across 11 steps. Now weigh it dispassionately and commit. Indecision at this stage is its own decision — and the worst one you can make.

  • →What still remains unvalidated or uncertain?
